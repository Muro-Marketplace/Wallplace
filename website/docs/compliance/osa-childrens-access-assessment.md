# Children's access assessment

**Service:** Wallplace (wallplace.co.uk)
**Provider:** Wallplace (sole trader, not yet incorporated)
**Duty:** Online Safety Act 2023 s.36
**Assessment carried out:** 10 September 2026
**Assessor:** Wallplace, with a technical audit of the live service
**Conclusion:** Wallplace is **not likely to be accessed by children**
**Next review:** 10 September 2027, or immediately on any of the triggers in section 7

> This assessment was produced late. Ofcom's guidance was published on 16 January 2025 with a completion deadline of 16 April 2025. Recording that plainly is part of the record; the assessment is now done and will be kept current.

---

## 1. The service

Wallplace is a UK marketplace connecting independent artists with commercial venues (cafes, restaurants, hotels, offices, bars) and with buyers. Artists list original physical artwork. Venues display it on their walls. Buyers purchase it, either online or by scanning a QR label next to a piece hanging in a venue.

At the date of this assessment the service has 47 registered accounts across four roles (artist, venue, customer, administrator), 19 completed orders and 91 placements. It has no mobile app.

## 3. Is the service, or part of it, of a kind likely to attract children?

**No.** The reasoning, rather than the conclusion:

- **The proposition is a purchase decision adults make.** Original artwork on the platform is priced in the tens to hundreds of pounds and is bought to hang on a wall in a home or a business. It is not a product children buy or ask for.
- **The other two roles are commercial.** Being an artist requires an application, an accepted subscription with a card, and Stripe Connect onboarding to receive payouts. Being a venue requires a commercial premises with wall space.
- **There is no child-appealing content or format.** No games, no video, no music, no avatars or characters, no gamification, no streaks, no rewards, no influencer content, no user-generated entertainment of any kind. The visual content is photographs of paintings, prints and drawings.
- **Nothing in the design targets or attracts children.** The tone, the typography and the copy are aimed at adults making a considered purchase or a commercial arrangement.
- **No cross-promotion from services children use.** There is no presence on platforms whose audience skews young, and no advertising anywhere.

The one part of the service that could in principle appeal more widely is the wall visualiser, which lets a user arrange artwork on a photograph of a wall. It is behind a login, it is metered by subscription tier, and it is a room-planning tool rather than a creative toy. It does not change the conclusion.

## 4. Is a significant number of children using the service?

**No.** Evidence rather than assumption:

- 47 registered accounts in total. Even if every one were a child, which the role requirements make implausible, the absolute number could not be significant.
- Registration in all three roles requires a verified email address, and the artist and venue roles require an application or a commercial premises.
- Payment for both artist subscriptions and artwork purchases requires a payment card.
- We hold no evidence of any child user, and no report has ever suggested one.

We do not profile users by age and do not intend to. That is a deliberate minimisation choice, and it means this limb rests on the structural argument above rather than on measured demographics. We consider that proportionate at this size, and section 7 says what would change it.

## 5. Conclusion

Wallplace is **not likely to be accessed by children** within the meaning of s.36. The children's risk assessment duty and the children's safety duties in Part 3 Chapter 2 therefore do not currently apply.

This conclusion is recorded, not assumed, and the reasoning above is what it rests on.

## 6. Measures in place regardless

The conclusion does not mean nothing is done. The following are in place whether or not the duty applies, and several of them are what would make the assessment easy to revisit:

- **An 18-or-over declaration at signup**, unticked and required, recorded against the terms acceptance. A declaration, not a verification, and deliberately so: stronger age assurance would be disproportionate for a service with no pornography and no children's product, and facial age estimation in particular would create biometric processing that Wallplace does not otherwise do.
- **The Terms and the Artist Agreement both require the account holder to be 18 or over**, and the Acceptable Use page says an account found to belong to someone under 18 is closed and its data deleted.
- **Privacy defaults that would already satisfy much of the ICO Children's code**: no profiling for advertising, no behavioural targeting, no advertising at all, geolocation coarsened to two decimal places before publication, no third-party analytics, no data sharing with advertisers, marketing off by default, and no nudge patterns toward lower-privacy settings.
- **Explicit content is prohibited** by the Acceptable Use page and enforced by the moderation and reporting process.

## 7. What would change this conclusion

This assessment is reviewed immediately, not at the annual date, if any of the following happens:

1. A feature is added that children would seek out: video, livestreaming, public commenting, group chat, gamification, or anything with the shape of a social feed.
2. Any content category is opened that is popular with under-18s, including prints or merchandise at a price point aimed at a younger buyer.
3. Marketing is directed at, or lands on, an audience that skews young, including any platform whose users are predominantly under 18.
4. A report, a support message, or any other evidence suggests a child is using the service.
5. The user base grows by an order of magnitude, at which point the structural argument in section 4 needs measured evidence behind it rather than reasoning.
6. Ofcom publishes revised guidance on the access test.
